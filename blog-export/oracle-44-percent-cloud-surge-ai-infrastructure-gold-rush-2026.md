Oracle reported Q3 fiscal 2026 earnings on March 10, and the numbers were striking enough to move the stock 9% in premarket trading the following morning. Cloud revenue hit $8.9 billion — up 44% year over year and ahead of analyst estimates. Cloud infrastructure revenue specifically grew 84% to $4.9 billion. Remaining Performance Obligations — contracted future revenue — jumped 325% to $553 billion, driven primarily by a $300 billion, five-year partnership with OpenAI as the centerpiece of Project Stargate. Oracle raised full-year fiscal 2027 revenue guidance to $90 billion. The company is spending $50 billion on capital expenditures in fiscal 2026 alone — building data centers in Texas, New Mexico, Michigan, Wisconsin, Wyoming, and Pennsylvania at a pace its own CEO described as demand outstripping supply. Oracle is no longer a legacy database software company that wandered into cloud. It is, as of this quarter, the landlord of the AI revolution — and the Q3 results make the case that the biggest infrastructure spending cycle in tech history is nowhere near its peak.

## The Company Nobody Thought Would Win the AI Infrastructure Race

If you had asked anyone in 2022 which cloud provider would emerge as the critical infrastructure backbone for the AI era, the answer would have been Amazon Web Services, Microsoft Azure, or Google Cloud. Probably AWS. Maybe Azure, given its OpenAI investment. Definitely not Oracle.

Oracle was founded in 1977. It spent forty years as the world's dominant relational database company. When the cloud era arrived, it was late — famously, embarrassingly late. Amazon launched AWS in 2006. Oracle didn't take cloud seriously until Larry Ellison stopped publicly dismissing it as a fad, which took until around 2012. By 2020, it was still an afterthought. AWS, Azure, and Google Cloud had consumed the market, and Oracle's cloud division was a rounding error on their quarterly reports.

What happened next is one of the stranger stories in enterprise technology. Oracle bet — deeply, expensively, and with an apparently alarming debt tolerance — on a single thesis: that AI training and inferencing would require so much raw compute capacity that the hyperscalers would be overwhelmed, and that a provider willing to build infrastructure specifically optimized for GPU workloads at massive scale could carve out a dominant position in the one segment that mattered most.

The Q3 2026 results are evidence that the thesis was correct.

## Breaking Down the Q3 Numbers

### The Headline Figures

Oracle's Q3 fiscal 2026 results showed total revenue up 22% to $17.2 billion, cloud revenue up 44% to $8.9 billion, and cloud infrastructure revenue up 84% to $4.9 billion. Remaining Performance Obligations — contracted future revenue — skyrocketed 325% to $553 billion. GAAP earnings per share rose 24% to $1.27.

Oracle noted this was the first quarter in over 15 years where both organic total revenue and non-GAAP earnings per share grew at 20% or more in USD simultaneously. That milestone matters because it signals the company's cloud transition has reached a scale where it is lifting the entire business — not just compensating for declining legacy revenue.

Cloud infrastructure revenue exploded 84% to $4.888 billion, driven by AI training and inferencing demand that Oracle says continues to outpace its own supply. That last phrase is significant. Oracle is not saying demand is strong. It is saying demand is so strong that it physically cannot build data centers fast enough to meet it.

### The Forward Guidance That Rewrote the Narrative

For Q4 fiscal 2026, Oracle guided total cloud revenue growth of 46% to 50% in USD. For fiscal year 2027, the company raised total revenue guidance to $90 billion.

$90 billion in fiscal 2027 revenue would represent a near-doubling of Oracle's total revenue in two years. For a company founded in 1977 — one that Wall Street spent a decade dismissing as trapped in a legacy business model — this is not a small claim.

Oracle CEO Safra Catz has projected that Oracle Cloud Infrastructure will grow 77% to $18 billion in fiscal 2026 and soar to $144 billion by 2030. A $144 billion infrastructure business by 2030 would make Oracle one of the largest cloud providers in the world by revenue — surpassing where Microsoft Azure and Google Cloud stand today.

### The Market's Response

Shares jumped roughly 8.7% in after-hours trading on March 10, closing the extended session at $162.40. The context matters: Oracle's stock entered the earnings report at $149.40, already down 1.43% on the day, well below its 200-day moving average, and roughly 50% off its September 2024 peak. The after-hours move was a sharp reversal, not a continuation of momentum — which tells you that even investors who had been skeptical of the Oracle thesis found the Q3 numbers difficult to dismiss.

## The $300 Billion Bet: Project Stargate and the OpenAI Partnership

The single largest driver behind Oracle's $553 billion backlog is its partnership with OpenAI through Project Stargate — a joint venture announced at the White House in January 2025 that has since grown into what multiple analysts describe as the largest infrastructure commitment in the history of computing.

### What Stargate Actually Is

The Stargate Project is a new company intending to invest $500 billion over the next four years building new AI infrastructure for OpenAI in the United States, with $100 billion deploying immediately. The initial equity funders are SoftBank, OpenAI, Oracle, and MGX, with SoftBank handling financial responsibility and OpenAI handling operational responsibility.

Oracle and OpenAI have entered an agreement to develop 4.5 gigawatts of additional Stargate data center capacity in the U.S. — bringing total Stargate capacity to over 5 gigawatts under development, which will run over 2 million chips.

In July 2025, OpenAI and Oracle entered an agreement to develop up to 4.5 gigawatts of additional Stargate capacity — a partnership that exceeds $300 billion between the two companies over the next five years. Five new U.S. data center sites have been announced: Shackelford County, Texas; Doña Ana County, New Mexico; Lordstown, Ohio; Milam County, Texas; and a midwestern site not yet publicly named, bringing Stargate to nearly 7 gigawatts of planned capacity and over $400 billion in investment over the next three years.

### The Physical Scale of What's Being Built

Under the five-year contract, Oracle is providing the physical infrastructure — land, power, and cooling — for a fleet of data centers housing over 2 million NVIDIA Blackwell-series GPUs. The flagship site in Abilene, Texas, known as Stargate I, is already operational, using innovative H-shaped hall designs specifically engineered for liquid-cooled GB200 racks. As of early 2026, Oracle is breaking ground on additional expansion sites across Michigan, Wisconsin, Wyoming, and Pennsylvania.

Oracle will ultimately deploy over 450,000 GB200 GPUs at the Abilene campus alone under a 15-year lease agreement, with the remaining six buildings expected to be completed by mid-2026. The full Abilene campus will use 1.2 gigawatts of power — enough for roughly one million homes.

To put 4.5 gigawatts of total Stargate capacity in perspective: that is roughly equivalent to the electricity output of two Hoover Dams, or enough to power approximately four million U.S. households. Oracle is not building data centers. It is building power infrastructure with data centers attached.

## Why Oracle Has an Advantage the Hyperscalers Don't

### The Multicloud Architecture That Matters

One of the less-discussed competitive advantages Oracle has developed through the AI boom is its multicloud positioning. Oracle Cloud Database multicloud revenue grew 531% year over year in Q3 — the single fastest-growing line item in the company's entire earnings report.

Multicloud database revenue growing 531% in a single quarter is not a rounding error. It reflects a fundamental shift in how enterprises are architecting their AI deployments. Rather than committing exclusively to one hyperscaler, large enterprises are running Oracle's database layer — where their most sensitive, most valuable structured data lives — on OCI, while running AI workloads across AWS, Azure, and Google Cloud simultaneously. Oracle sits in the middle as the connective tissue.

This is architecturally significant because it gives Oracle leverage no pure-play infrastructure provider has: it controls the data layer. Enterprises don't leave their database vendors easily, particularly when those databases hold decades of transactional records. Oracle's cloud transition, viewed through this lens, is not just about competing with AWS on infrastructure. It's about extending database lock-in into the AI era.

### GPU Supply Secured When Others Were Asleep

Oracle's investment includes approximately $40 billion to buy around 400,000 of NVIDIA's latest AI accelerators in the GB200 series. This procurement happened at scale before the GPU supply crunch reached its current severity. Oracle locked in delivery schedules and pricing when NVIDIA's order books were less crowded. The hyperscalers — AWS, Azure, Google — are all fighting for the same GPU supply Oracle has already committed contracts to receive.

The practical result is that Oracle can promise customers delivery timelines that competitors currently cannot match. For an AI company training a frontier model that needs 100,000 GPUs in six months, the provider that can actually deliver on schedule has enormous leverage.

## The Risk Embedded Inside Every Number

It would be dishonest to write about Oracle's Q3 results without acknowledging the financial risk that sits underneath the growth story.

Oracle carries a total debt-to-equity ratio of 427%, compared to Microsoft's 32%. Its investments in AI infrastructure already exceed its cash flow. The company is spending $50 billion on capital expenditures in fiscal 2026 alone — building on a debt load that has ballooned past $100 billion to finance the Stargate buildout.

The primary challenge for the remainder of 2026 and 2027 will be operational execution. Any delays in power delivery or hardware installation could result in significant penalties or missed revenue targets. For OpenAI, the challenge is equally steep — it must successfully monetize its next-generation models to justify the $60 billion annual payments that will begin flowing to Oracle from 2028.

There is also a concentration risk that deserves naming clearly. A substantial portion of Oracle's $553 billion RPO backlog flows from contracts with a small number of AI companies — with OpenAI as the anchor. OpenAI remains far from profitability, is burning through significant capital, and CEO Sam Altman has told investors the company may require up to $44 billion to reach profitability no sooner than 2029. If OpenAI's growth trajectory stalls, or if the AI infrastructure demand cycle peaks earlier than Oracle's 2030 projections assume, Oracle's debt-financed buildout becomes a liability rather than an asset.

The bull case and the bear case are separated by one question: does AI infrastructure demand continue to compound at the rate the $553 billion backlog implies? The Q3 results are the best evidence yet that it does. But "continues to outpace our supply" is a description of today's order books — not a guarantee about 2028's.

## What This Means for the AI Infrastructure Landscape

Oracle's Q3 results have two meanings simultaneously.

The first meaning is specific to Oracle. The company completed one of the most improbable pivots in enterprise tech history — from a legacy database vendor that nearly missed the cloud era entirely to the physical infrastructure backbone of the largest AI infrastructure project ever attempted. Cloud now accounts for roughly 52% of Oracle's total sales, up from approximately 43% a year ago — a decisive shift from a decade ago when Oracle was largely defined by its on-premise database business.

The second meaning is broader. Oracle's numbers — and the fact that its own CEO says demand is outpacing supply — are evidence that the AI infrastructure investment cycle is not winding down. Every major tech company has been asked by analysts for the past eighteen months whether the data center buildout is sustainable, whether demand justifies the capital expenditure, whether the gold rush will end. Oracle's $553 billion backlog is not a projection. It is contracted revenue. Customers have already signed. The money is already committed.

The gold rush is not ending. The contracts are in place. The shovels are in the ground. The only question now is whether what gets built is worth what it costs — and the answer to that question won't be clear for another two years at minimum.

## FAQ

**What are Remaining Performance Obligations (RPO) and why does $553 billion matter?**
RPO is the total value of contracted revenue that Oracle has not yet recognized — money customers have already committed to spending but that hasn't been earned yet because the services haven't been delivered. A 325% year-over-year increase to $553 billion means Oracle's pipeline of already-signed future work grew more than four times over in a single year. It is one of the strongest forward-looking signals available in enterprise technology reporting.

**What is Project Stargate?**
Stargate is a joint venture announced at the White House in January 2025, funded by OpenAI, SoftBank, Oracle, and Abu Dhabi's MGX. It plans to invest $500 billion over four years building AI data center infrastructure across the United States. Oracle is the primary infrastructure provider, building data centers that house NVIDIA Blackwell GPU clusters. The flagship site in Abilene, Texas, is already operational. Five additional sites have been announced across Texas, New Mexico, Ohio, and the Midwest.

**How does Oracle's cloud business compare to AWS, Azure, and Google Cloud?**
By absolute revenue, Oracle's $8.9 billion quarterly cloud revenue is smaller than the three hyperscalers. AWS reported $29.6 billion in Q4 2024; Azure and Google Cloud each reported approximately $11–12 billion per quarter. The difference is growth rate: Oracle's 44% cloud revenue growth and 84% infrastructure growth outpaces all three hyperscalers. At current growth rates, Oracle is closing the gap meaningfully — and in the AI infrastructure segment specifically, its contracted backlog rivals what the hyperscalers have publicly disclosed.

**Is Oracle's debt level a serious risk?**
Yes. Oracle's total debt-to-equity ratio of 427% is dramatically higher than peers like Microsoft (32%) and Google. The company is spending $50 billion in capital expenditures in fiscal 2026 — more than its annual operating cash flow — and has financed the gap with debt. The risk is manageable as long as AI infrastructure demand stays strong and contracted customers fulfill their commitments. It becomes a serious problem if demand flattens faster than Oracle's payment schedules require. Investors should monitor quarterly RPO changes as the clearest leading indicator of whether demand assumptions are holding.

**What does "demand outpacing supply" actually mean for Oracle customers?**
It means Oracle's existing data center capacity is fully committed and customers who want GPU capacity from Oracle Cloud Infrastructure are being placed on waiting lists. Oracle is building as fast as it can — breaking ground on multiple new campuses simultaneously — but physical construction and power infrastructure take 18 to 24 months to bring online. Customers who need capacity now are signing long-term contracts to guarantee future allocations, which is exactly why the RPO number is growing faster than current revenue.

**Why did Larry Ellison briefly become the richest person in the world?**
When Oracle reported its Q1 fiscal 2026 earnings in September 2025 — which first revealed the scale of the Stargate-related backlog — the stock surged 43% in a single day. Ellison, who holds a substantial stake in Oracle, saw his personal fortune jump by nearly $90 billion, briefly reaching $383 billion and placing him ahead of Elon Musk. The moment lasted hours before markets adjusted. It was, however, a vivid illustration of what a $553 billion contracted backlog does to the net worth of a founder who still owns a large slice of his company.
