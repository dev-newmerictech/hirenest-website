## 1. Why Most Manager Interviews Fail

Hiring a manager is one of the highest-stakes decisions a company makes. A great manager multiplies team performance. A poor manager destroys it — through micromanagement, poor communication, inconsistent feedback, or an inability to develop people.

Yet most companies interview managers the same way they interview individual contributors: with generic questions about experience, strengths, and weaknesses. These questions don't reveal whether someone can actually lead.

The research on this is unambiguous. Unstructured interviews — where interviewers ask whatever comes to mind — have a predictive validity of 0.20 on a 0–1 scale. That means they predict job performance only slightly better than chance. Structured behavioral interviews, by contrast, have a predictive validity of 0.51 — more than twice as predictive.

The difference is not the interviewer. It is the questions. Behavioral interview questions — questions that ask candidates to describe specific past situations — are dramatically more predictive of future performance than hypothetical questions or general experience questions.

This guide gives you the behavioral interview questions that predict management success, organized by competency, with scoring rubrics and red flags for each.

## 2. The STAR+ Framework for Behavioral Interviews

Before we get to the questions, let's establish the framework for evaluating answers.

The standard framework for behavioral interviews is STAR: Situation, Task, Action, Result. The candidate describes a specific situation, the task they faced, the actions they took, and the result they achieved.

For management interviews, we add two layers: **Reflection** and **Verification**.

**STAR+ = Situation + Task + Action + Result + Reflection + Verification**

- **Reflection:** "What would you do differently if you faced this situation again?" This reveals self-awareness and growth mindset — critical management competencies.
- **Verification:** "How did you measure the result?" or "What did your team say about this?" This probes for specificity and honesty — candidates who are exaggerating or fabricating will struggle to answer verification questions.

### How to Use STAR+ in Practice

When a candidate gives a STAR answer, probe with:
- "What specifically did you do?" (if the Action is vague)
- "How did you measure that result?" (Verification)
- "What would you do differently?" (Reflection)
- "What did your team think about this approach?" (Verification + team perspective)

The goal is not to catch candidates out — it is to get enough specific evidence to make an accurate assessment.

## 3. The 8 Core Management Competencies

Great managers are strong in 8 core competencies. Your interview should evaluate all 8 — though not every question needs to cover every competency.

1. **Team Development:** Ability to identify, develop, and retain talent
2. **Communication:** Ability to communicate clearly, listen actively, and adapt style to audience
3. **Conflict Resolution:** Ability to address and resolve interpersonal and performance conflicts
4. **Decision-Making:** Ability to make sound decisions under uncertainty and with incomplete information
5. **Delegation:** Ability to assign work effectively and develop team members through delegation
6. **Performance Management:** Ability to set clear expectations, provide feedback, and manage underperformance
7. **Adaptability:** Ability to lead through change, ambiguity, and adversity
8. **Strategic Thinking:** Ability to connect day-to-day work to broader organizational goals

## 4. Behavioral Interview Questions by Competency

### Competency 1: Team Development

**Question 1:** "Tell me about the team member you're most proud of developing. What was their starting point, what did you do, and where are they now?"

*What you're looking for:* Specific actions (not just "I coached them"), measurable progress, genuine investment in the person's growth.

*Red flags:* Vague answers ("I gave them feedback and they improved"), inability to name specific development actions, taking full credit for the person's growth without acknowledging their own effort.

*Scoring rubric:*
- **5 (Exceptional):** Describes a specific development plan with concrete actions, measurable milestones, and a clear outcome. Shows genuine pride in the person's success.
- **4 (Strong):** Describes specific actions and a positive outcome, but less systematic or measurable.
- **3 (Adequate):** Describes a development story but actions are vague or the outcome is unclear.
- **2 (Weak):** Struggles to identify a specific example or describes only passive support ("I was available if they had questions").
- **1 (Poor):** Cannot identify a team member they've developed, or describes a negative development experience without reflection.

---

**Question 2:** "Tell me about a time you had to let someone go. How did you approach it, and what did you learn?"

*What you're looking for:* A structured, humane approach to a difficult conversation; evidence that the decision was not made impulsively; reflection on what they could have done differently earlier.

*Red flags:* Describing the termination as a surprise to the employee (suggests poor ongoing feedback), blaming the employee entirely without self-reflection, discomfort discussing the topic.

*Scoring rubric:*
- **5 (Exceptional):** Describes a clear process (documentation, prior feedback, PIP if applicable), a respectful conversation, and genuine reflection on what they could have done differently.
- **4 (Strong):** Describes a structured approach and shows empathy, but less reflection on their own role.
- **3 (Adequate):** Handled the situation but process was less structured or the conversation was poorly managed.
- **2 (Weak):** Describes a termination that was a surprise to the employee, or handled the conversation poorly.
- **1 (Poor):** Cannot describe a termination experience, or describes one that was clearly mishandled.

---

### Competency 2: Communication

**Question 3:** "Tell me about a time you had to communicate a difficult message to your team — a layoff, a strategy change, or bad news about the business. How did you approach it?"

*What you're looking for:* Transparency, empathy, preparation, and follow-through. Great managers don't sugarcoat difficult messages — they communicate them clearly and create space for questions and emotions.

*Red flags:* Describing a communication that was vague or delayed ("I waited until I had all the information"), failing to acknowledge the emotional impact on the team, or describing a communication that was primarily about managing their own discomfort.

*Scoring rubric:*
- **5 (Exceptional):** Describes a clear, timely, empathetic communication. Created space for questions. Followed up individually with team members who were most affected.
- **4 (Strong):** Communicated clearly and empathetically, but less structured follow-through.
- **3 (Adequate):** Communicated the message but timing or delivery was suboptimal.
- **2 (Weak):** Delayed the communication or delivered it poorly, with limited awareness of the impact.
- **1 (Poor):** Cannot describe a difficult communication, or describes one that was clearly mishandled.

---

**Question 4:** "Tell me about a time you had to adapt your communication style for a specific team member. What did you change, and why?"

*What you're looking for:* Awareness that different people communicate differently, and the flexibility to adapt. Great managers don't communicate the same way with everyone — they adjust their style based on what works for each person.

*Red flags:* Inability to identify a specific example, or describing a one-size-fits-all communication approach.

---

### Competency 3: Conflict Resolution

**Question 5:** "Tell me about a time two members of your team had a significant conflict. How did you handle it?"

*What you're looking for:* A structured approach to conflict resolution — understanding both perspectives, facilitating a direct conversation, reaching a resolution, and following up. Great managers don't avoid conflict; they address it directly and constructively.

*Red flags:* Avoiding the conflict ("I let them work it out"), taking sides without understanding both perspectives, or describing a resolution that didn't stick.

*Scoring rubric:*
- **5 (Exceptional):** Describes a structured approach: met with each person individually, facilitated a joint conversation, reached a clear resolution, followed up to ensure the resolution held.
- **4 (Strong):** Addressed the conflict directly and reached a resolution, but less structured process.
- **3 (Adequate):** Addressed the conflict but process was reactive rather than structured.
- **2 (Weak):** Avoided the conflict or handled it in a way that didn't resolve the underlying issue.
- **1 (Poor):** Cannot describe a conflict resolution experience, or describes one that was clearly mishandled.

---

**Question 6:** "Tell me about a time you disagreed with a decision made by your manager or leadership. What did you do?"

*What you're looking for:* The ability to advocate for their perspective through appropriate channels, and then commit to the decision once it's made — even if they disagree. This is "disagree and commit" — a critical management competency.

*Red flags:* Describing passive compliance without advocacy ("I just did what I was told"), or describing ongoing resistance after the decision was made ("I kept pushing back even after the decision was final").

---

### Competency 4: Decision-Making

**Question 7:** "Tell me about the most difficult decision you've made as a manager. What made it difficult, and how did you approach it?"

*What you're looking for:* A structured decision-making process, comfort with ambiguity, and the ability to make a decision and move forward even when the outcome is uncertain.

*Red flags:* Describing a decision that was not actually difficult (suggests limited experience), or describing a decision-making process that was purely intuitive with no structured analysis.

*Scoring rubric:*
- **5 (Exceptional):** Describes a genuinely difficult decision with a clear process: gathered relevant information, consulted stakeholders, weighed trade-offs, made a decision, and communicated it clearly. Reflects on what they learned.
- **4 (Strong):** Describes a difficult decision with a structured approach, but less reflection on the process.
- **3 (Adequate):** Describes a difficult decision but the process was less structured or the outcome is unclear.
- **2 (Weak):** Describes a decision that was not particularly difficult, or a process that was purely reactive.
- **1 (Poor):** Cannot describe a difficult decision, or describes one that was clearly mishandled.

---

**Question 8:** "Tell me about a time you made a decision that turned out to be wrong. What happened, and what did you do?"

*What you're looking for:* Accountability, self-awareness, and the ability to course-correct. Great managers own their mistakes — they don't blame circumstances or team members.

*Red flags:* Inability to identify a decision that was wrong, or describing a mistake that was entirely attributed to external factors.

---

### Competency 5: Delegation

**Question 9:** "Tell me about a time you delegated a significant responsibility to a team member who had never done it before. How did you set them up for success?"

*What you're looking for:* A structured approach to delegation — clear expectations, appropriate support, regular check-ins, and the ability to let go without micromanaging.

*Red flags:* Describing delegation as "just assigning tasks," inability to describe how they supported the team member, or describing a delegation that failed because of insufficient support.

*Scoring rubric:*
- **5 (Exceptional):** Describes a clear delegation process: defined the outcome (not just the task), provided relevant context and resources, established check-in cadence, gave feedback along the way, and celebrated the team member's success.
- **4 (Strong):** Delegated effectively with good support, but less structured process.
- **3 (Adequate):** Delegated the responsibility but support was insufficient or the check-in cadence was too infrequent.
- **2 (Weak):** Describes delegation as task assignment with minimal support.
- **1 (Poor):** Cannot describe a delegation experience, or describes one where they micromanaged or took the task back.

---

**Question 10:** "Tell me about a time you struggled to let go of a task or responsibility. What was it, and how did you handle it?"

*What you're looking for:* Self-awareness about the tendency to hold on to work, and the ability to recognize and correct this pattern. Great managers know that their job is to make their team successful — not to do the work themselves.

*Red flags:* Inability to identify a task they struggled to delegate, or describing a pattern of holding on to work without recognizing it as a problem.

---

### Competency 6: Performance Management

**Question 11:** "Tell me about a time you had to manage a team member who was underperforming. What did you do, and what was the outcome?"

*What you're looking for:* A structured approach to performance management — clear expectations, specific feedback, a performance improvement plan (if appropriate), and a clear outcome (improvement or exit).

*Red flags:* Avoiding the performance conversation until it was too late, giving vague feedback ("I told them they needed to do better"), or describing a performance management process that was punitive rather than developmental.

*Scoring rubric:*
- **5 (Exceptional):** Describes a clear process: identified the performance gap early, had a direct conversation with specific feedback, created a structured improvement plan with measurable milestones, followed up regularly, and reached a clear outcome (improvement or exit).
- **4 (Strong):** Managed the performance issue directly with specific feedback, but less structured process.
- **3 (Adequate):** Addressed the performance issue but timing was late or feedback was insufficiently specific.
- **2 (Weak):** Avoided the performance conversation or gave vague feedback.
- **1 (Poor):** Cannot describe a performance management experience, or describes one that was clearly mishandled.

---

**Question 12:** "Tell me about a time you had to give feedback that you knew the person wouldn't want to hear. How did you approach it?"

*What you're looking for:* The ability to deliver difficult feedback directly, specifically, and constructively — without softening it to the point of ineffectiveness.

*Red flags:* Describing feedback that was so softened it wasn't heard ("I tried to be gentle"), or feedback that was delivered in a group setting or via email rather than directly.

---

### Competency 7: Adaptability

**Question 13:** "Tell me about a time your team had to go through a significant change — a reorganization, a strategy shift, a new system, or a major process change. How did you lead them through it?"

*What you're looking for:* The ability to communicate change clearly, address team concerns, maintain morale, and keep the team productive during the transition.

*Red flags:* Describing a change that was poorly communicated, or a leader who was not transparent about the reasons for the change.

*Scoring rubric:*
- **5 (Exceptional):** Communicated the change clearly and early, explained the rationale, acknowledged the impact on the team, created space for questions and concerns, maintained morale, and kept the team productive throughout the transition.
- **4 (Strong):** Led the team through the change effectively, but communication or morale management was less structured.
- **3 (Adequate):** Managed the change but communication was delayed or incomplete.
- **2 (Weak):** Struggled to communicate the change or maintain team morale.
- **1 (Poor):** Cannot describe a change management experience, or describes one that was clearly mishandled.

---

**Question 14:** "Tell me about a time you had to lead your team through a period of significant uncertainty — when you didn't have all the answers. How did you handle it?"

*What you're looking for:* The ability to be transparent about uncertainty while maintaining team confidence and direction. Great managers don't pretend to have answers they don't have — but they also don't let uncertainty paralyze the team.

*Red flags:* Pretending to have answers they didn't have, or allowing uncertainty to create team paralysis.

---

### Competency 8: Strategic Thinking

**Question 15:** "Tell me about a time you identified a significant opportunity or problem that your team or organization wasn't aware of. What did you do?"

*What you're looking for:* The ability to see beyond day-to-day execution to identify patterns, trends, and opportunities. Great managers don't just execute — they think.

*Red flags:* Inability to identify a strategic insight they've contributed, or describing a "strategic" insight that was actually tactical.

*Scoring rubric:*
- **5 (Exceptional):** Describes a specific insight that was not obvious, explains how they identified it (data, customer feedback, market observation), describes how they communicated it to leadership, and describes the impact.
- **4 (Strong):** Describes a strategic insight with clear impact, but the identification process is less systematic.
- **3 (Adequate):** Describes a strategic contribution but it is more tactical than strategic.
- **2 (Weak):** Struggles to identify a strategic contribution.
- **1 (Poor):** Cannot describe a strategic contribution.

---

**Question 16:** "Tell me about a time you had to prioritize between multiple important initiatives. How did you decide what to focus on?"

*What you're looking for:* A structured prioritization framework, the ability to make trade-offs explicitly, and the ability to communicate priorities clearly to the team.

*Red flags:* Describing prioritization as "just doing the most urgent thing," or inability to describe how they communicated priorities to the team.

## 5. Questions to Avoid — and What to Ask Instead

### Questions That Are Illegal

- "Do you have children?" → Ask: "Are you able to meet the travel requirements of this role?"
- "Where are you from originally?" → Don't ask. Irrelevant to job performance.
- "How old are you?" → Don't ask. Illegal in most jurisdictions.
- "Are you planning to have children?" → Don't ask. Illegal in most jurisdictions.
- "Do you have any disabilities?" → Ask: "Are you able to perform the essential functions of this role with or without reasonable accommodation?"

### Questions That Are Ineffective

- "What is your greatest weakness?" → Ask: "Tell me about a time you received critical feedback. How did you respond?"
- "Where do you see yourself in 5 years?" → Ask: "What are you trying to learn or develop in your next role?"
- "Why should we hire you?" → Ask: "What specifically about this role and company makes it the right next step for you?"
- "Tell me about yourself." → Ask: "Walk me through the management experience that's most relevant to this role."

## 6. The Complete 60-Minute Manager Interview

Here is a complete 60-minute interview structure for a management role, using the questions above.

**Minutes 0–5: Introduction and framing**
- Welcome the candidate and introduce yourself
- Explain the interview structure: "We'll spend most of our time on behavioral questions — I'll ask you to describe specific situations from your experience. I'm looking for concrete examples, not hypotheticals."
- Ask the candidate if they have any questions before you begin

**Minutes 5–20: Team development and communication (2 questions)**
- Question 1: Team development (Q1 or Q2 above)
- Question 2: Communication (Q3 or Q4 above)

**Minutes 20–35: Conflict and decision-making (2 questions)**
- Question 3: Conflict resolution (Q5 or Q6 above)
- Question 4: Decision-making (Q7 or Q8 above)

**Minutes 35–50: Performance management and delegation (2 questions)**
- Question 5: Performance management (Q11 or Q12 above)
- Question 6: Delegation (Q9 or Q10 above)

**Minutes 50–60: Candidate questions**
- "What questions do you have for me?"
- Listen carefully — the questions a candidate asks reveal what they care about and how they think.

## 7. Scoring and Decision-Making

### The Scorecard

After each interview, complete a scorecard that rates the candidate on each of the 8 competencies on a 1–5 scale, with specific evidence for each rating.

**Competency ratings:**
- Team Development: __ / 5 (Evidence: ___)
- Communication: __ / 5 (Evidence: ___)
- Conflict Resolution: __ / 5 (Evidence: ___)
- Decision-Making: __ / 5 (Evidence: ___)
- Delegation: __ / 5 (Evidence: ___)
- Performance Management: __ / 5 (Evidence: ___)
- Adaptability: __ / 5 (Evidence: ___)
- Strategic Thinking: __ / 5 (Evidence: ___)

**Overall score:** __ / 40

**Overall recommendation:** Strong Hire / Hire / No Hire / Strong No Hire

### Interpreting the Scores

- **35–40:** Exceptional candidate. Move quickly — they likely have multiple offers.
- **28–34:** Strong candidate. Worth advancing to the next round.
- **20–27:** Adequate candidate. Consider advancing if the pipeline is thin, but probe specific weak areas in the next round.
- **Below 20:** Not a fit for this role. Reject with a specific, honest explanation.

### The Debrief

After all interviewers have submitted their scorecards, hold a 30-minute debrief:

1. **Share scores (10 minutes):** Each interviewer shares their overall score and the specific evidence behind their ratings. No discussion yet.
2. **Discuss disagreements (15 minutes):** Focus on evidence, not impressions. "I scored them 2/5 on performance management because they couldn't describe a specific PIP they'd created" is useful. "I just didn't get a good vibe" is not.
3. **Decision (5 minutes):** Hire, no hire, or "we need one more data point" (which should be rare and specific).

## 8. Red Flags in Manager Interviews

Beyond the specific question-level red flags, watch for these overall red flags in manager interviews:

**1. Taking all the credit.** A manager who says "I did X" when they mean "my team did X" is a red flag. Great managers talk about their team's accomplishments, not their own.

**2. Blaming team members.** A manager who describes team failures as the team's fault — without any reflection on their own role — is a red flag. Great managers own their team's performance.

**3. Vague answers.** A manager who can't describe specific situations, specific actions, and specific results is either inexperienced or exaggerating. Probe for specificity.

**4. No examples of difficult conversations.** A manager who can't describe a difficult feedback conversation, a performance management situation, or a conflict resolution is either inexperienced or conflict-avoidant. Both are red flags.

**5. Inconsistency.** If a candidate's answers are inconsistent — describing themselves as a "hands-off" manager in one answer and a "very involved" manager in another — probe for the inconsistency. It may indicate that they are telling you what they think you want to hear.

**6. No questions.** A candidate who has no questions for you at the end of the interview is either not genuinely interested in the role or not thinking critically about the opportunity. Both are red flags.

## 9. Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How many behavioral questions should I ask in a manager interview?

**A:** 4–6 behavioral questions per 60-minute interview is the right range. Fewer than 4 doesn't give you enough data. More than 6 and you won't have time to probe deeply enough to get useful evidence.

### Q: What if a candidate doesn't have a relevant example?

**A:** If a candidate can't provide a relevant example for a core management competency, that is itself a data point. Probe gently: "Can you think of a situation where you faced something similar, even if it wasn't exactly the same?" If they still can't, score them 1–2 on that competency and note it in the scorecard.

### Q: How do I evaluate a first-time manager who doesn't have management experience?

**A:** Look for examples from other contexts — leading a project, mentoring a junior colleague, running a volunteer organization. The specific management experience matters less than the underlying competencies. A first-time manager who has demonstrated strong communication, conflict resolution, and development skills in other contexts is more likely to succeed than an experienced manager who has demonstrated poor skills.

### Q: Should I use the same questions for every management candidate?

**A:** Yes — for consistency. Using the same questions for every candidate allows you to compare candidates against each other, not just against an abstract standard. It also reduces the risk of bias (interviewers asking easier questions to candidates they like).

## 10. Glossary

**Behavioral Interview:** An interview format in which candidates are asked to describe specific past situations, rather than hypothetical scenarios or general experience.

**Calibration Session:** A structured discussion among interviewers before the hiring process begins, designed to align on evaluation standards and scoring rubrics.

**Competency:** A specific skill, behavior, or characteristic that is required for success in a role.

**Disagree and Commit:** The ability to advocate for one's perspective through appropriate channels, and then commit to the decision once it's made — even if one disagrees.

**Performance Improvement Plan (PIP):** A structured document that outlines specific performance expectations, a timeline for improvement, and consequences if improvement is not achieved.

**Predictive Validity:** A statistical measure of how well a hiring assessment predicts actual job performance. Structured behavioral interviews: 0.51. Unstructured interviews: 0.20.

**Scorecard:** A structured evaluation form that rates a candidate on specific competencies, with space for evidence and an overall recommendation.

**STAR Framework:** A framework for behavioral interview answers: Situation, Task, Action, Result.

**STAR+ Framework:** An enhanced version of STAR that adds Reflection ("What would you do differently?") and Verification ("How did you measure that result?").
