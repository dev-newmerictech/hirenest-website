## The Biggest Seasonal Hiring Mistakes (And How to Avoid Them)

**Starting too late.** Most seasonal employers begin recruiting when they are already short-staffed. The best seasonal workers are hired 6–8 weeks before peak season — not 2 weeks before.

**Vague about the end date.** Candidates who accept seasonal roles without a clear end date are more likely to leave abruptly when personal circumstances change. Be explicit: "This is a seasonal role from May 15 through September 1."

**Treating seasonal workers as less than full employees.** Seasonal employees have the same legal rights as permanent employees. Skipping I-9s, W-4s, and payroll taxes for seasonal workers creates serious legal exposure.

**Not creating a returnable pool.** The highest ROI activity in seasonal hiring is bringing back strong workers from previous seasons. This requires deliberately building relationships and maintaining records — most employers do not do this.

## Step 1: Plan Your Needs Before You Post

Before recruiting, define:

**How many people do you need?**
Overestimating leads to excess payroll. Underestimating leads to a crisis. Look at last season's volume data and build in a 10–15% buffer.

**What are the exact dates?**
Start date, end date, and whether there is any flexibility. Be honest about this in your posting.

**What hours and schedule?**
Seasonal work often involves irregular or intensive hours. Candidates need to know if weekends, evenings, or 50+ hour weeks are part of the expectation.

**What is the pay rate?**
Seasonal workers often compare multiple short-term opportunities simultaneously. A competitive rate — and communicating it clearly — is essential for attracting quality applicants quickly.

## Step 2: Where to Find Seasonal Workers

**Indeed and ZipRecruiter:** Both have seasonal job filters that candidates actively use. Tag your posting as seasonal and specify the exact dates.

**College and university job boards:** Students actively seeking summer or holiday work are a strong seasonal talent pool. Contact career services offices at local colleges.

**High school career centers:** For roles appropriate for high school students (retail, food service, outdoor labor), career counselors at local high schools can send candidates your way.

**Previous seasonal employees:** This is your best source. Reach out to workers from previous seasons directly — before posting publicly. A personal message or call costs nothing and often brings back your best people.

**Staffing agencies:** For high-volume seasonal needs (warehouse, agriculture, events), staffing agencies handle recruiting, screening, and often payroll. You pay a markup (typically 20–40% above the worker's hourly rate) but save enormous administrative time.

**Local community platforms:** Nextdoor, Facebook community groups, and local Reddit communities are effective for neighborhood-based seasonal businesses.

## Step 3: Screen Quickly Without Sacrificing Quality

Seasonal hiring requires speed, but speed without screening leads to high no-show rates and early departures. A fast but structured approach:

**Knock-out application questions:**
Add 2–3 required questions to your application: exact availability dates, ability to commit to the full season, any relevant certifications or requirements. This filters mismatches before you spend any time reviewing.

**Brief phone screen (10 minutes):**
Confirm availability and dates match, the candidate understands the role is seasonal, they can meet the physical or schedule requirements, and they seem reliable and communicative.

**One structured interview (30 minutes):**
For most seasonal roles, a single 30-minute structured interview is sufficient. Focus on:
- Reliability signals (tenure at previous jobs, even short-term ones)
- How they have handled physically demanding or fast-paced work before
- Why they want a seasonal role specifically (looking for temporary income is fine; being unable to get a permanent job is worth exploring)
- Whether they would be interested in returning next season if it goes well

## Step 4: Legal Requirements for Seasonal Employees

Seasonal employees are employees. The same legal requirements apply:

**Federal requirements:**
- Form I-9 (employment eligibility) — complete within 3 business days of start
- Form W-4 (tax withholding)
- Payroll taxes (Social Security, Medicare, federal unemployment)

**State requirements:**
- State income tax withholding (where applicable)
- State unemployment insurance (SUTA)
- New hire reporting

**Workers' compensation:** Required in most states for all employees including seasonal. Confirm your policy covers seasonal workers — some policies have restrictions.

**Minimum wage and overtime:** Seasonal workers are entitled to federal and state minimum wage and overtime pay (1.5x for hours over 40 per week) unless a specific exemption applies. Agricultural seasonal workers have specific exemptions under FLSA — consult an employment attorney if this applies.

## Step 5: Onboard Seasonal Workers for a Fast Start

Seasonal workers do not have the luxury of a slow learning curve. Your onboarding needs to get them productive within days, not weeks.

**Day 1 must accomplish:**
- All legal paperwork completed
- Understanding of their specific role and daily responsibilities
- Introduction to their direct supervisor and key colleagues
- Safety training (especially for physical roles)
- Clear communication about expectations, schedule, and how to raise questions

**Week 1 goal:**
The seasonal employee should be operating independently in their core responsibilities by the end of the first week. If they are not, identify the gap (training, fit, or role clarity) and address it immediately.

---

## Step 6: Build a Returnables Pool

The highest-value thing you can do in seasonal hiring is turn one-season workers into multi-season returners:

**At end of season:**
- Have a brief wrap-up conversation with every seasonal employee who performed well
- Ask directly: "Would you be interested in coming back next season if the role is available?"
- Record their contact information in a separate list labeled "Seasonal Returnables"

**Before next season:**
- Contact returnables 8–10 weeks before your season starts — before you post publicly
- Offer them first right of refusal on the role

Employers who return 50–70% of seasonal staff from the prior year spend a fraction of what first-time seasonal employers spend on recruiting and training each season.

## FAQ

**Do I need to pay seasonal workers overtime?**
Yes, in most cases. Seasonal employees are subject to the same overtime rules as permanent employees — 1.5x pay for hours over 40 per week under the FLSA. Some specific agricultural and camp counselor exemptions exist, but most seasonal workers in retail, hospitality, and general labor are entitled to overtime.

**Can I classify seasonal workers as independent contractors to avoid payroll taxes?**
Only if they genuinely meet the legal definition of an independent contractor — which most seasonal workers do not. The IRS and state agencies look closely at worker classification. Misclassifying employees as contractors is a significant legal risk with back taxes, penalties, and interest as consequences.

**How far in advance should I start seasonal hiring?**
For most seasonal businesses, 6–8 weeks before your peak season starts. For high-volume seasonal operations (large agriculture, major hospitality venues, large retailers), 10–12 weeks is more appropriate.

**What is the best way to retain seasonal workers through the full season?**
Clear communication of expectations from day one, prompt resolution of issues, consistent scheduling, and recognizing good work publicly. Mid-season check-ins (around week 4–5) where you ask "how is it going, what do you need" reduce early departures significantly.

**Can I convert a seasonal worker to a permanent employee?**
Yes — and it is often one of the best ways to hire. A seasonal employee who performs well has already been vetted in your environment. Converting strong seasonal performers to permanent roles saves you recruiting costs and brings in someone already trained in your operation.
