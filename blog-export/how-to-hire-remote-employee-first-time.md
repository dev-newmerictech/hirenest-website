## Remote Hiring Is Not Just In-Person Hiring With a Laptop

Most employers approach remote hiring the same way they approach in-person hiring — and then wonder why remote employees are harder to manage, slower to ramp up, and more likely to leave within 12 months.

Remote work requires a specific skill set that has nothing to do with technical competence: the ability to work without external structure, communicate proactively across time zones, manage time without supervision, and stay motivated in an isolated environment. Screening for these traits is different from screening for job skills.

This guide covers both the legal complexity and the hiring process for remote employees.

## Part 1: The Legal Complexity of Remote Hiring

### You Must Register in the Employee's State

When you hire a remote employee, they create what is called "nexus" in their state — meaning your business has a presence there for legal and tax purposes. This typically requires:

- Registering your business to operate in that state
- Setting up state income tax withholding for that state
- Registering for that state's unemployment insurance
- Complying with that state's specific employment laws

This applies even if you are a sole proprietor or small LLC. Hiring a remote employee in California, New York, or any other state without registering there first creates legal and tax exposure.

**Action step:** Before extending an offer to a remote employee in a new state, consult a payroll service (Gusto, Rippling, or Deel handle multi-state compliance automatically) or a business attorney.

### State-Specific Employment Laws Vary Significantly

California, New York, Colorado, Massachusetts, and Illinois have significantly more employee-protective laws than other states. These include:

- Stricter rules around final paychecks (California requires same-day payment on termination)
- Paid family and medical leave requirements
- Specific wage and hour rules
- Stricter non-compete enforceability restrictions

What is legal employer behavior in Texas may create liability in California. Know the rules in your remote employee's state.

### Use a Payroll Service That Handles Multi-State Compliance

Do not try to manage multi-state payroll manually. Services like Gusto, Rippling, Deel, or ADP handle state registrations, tax withholding, compliance notices, and new hire reporting automatically. The cost ($40–$100/month) is minimal compared to the risk of non-compliance.

## Part 2: Interviewing for Remote Work Fit

### The 5 Traits That Predict Remote Work Success

Before assessing job skills, assess these:

**1. Self-direction:** Can they set their own priorities and execute without being told what to do each day?

**2. Proactive communication:** Do they reach out before a problem gets large, or do they wait to be asked?

**3. Async discipline:** Can they work effectively when their manager is in a different time zone or unavailable for hours?

**4. Written communication:** Remote work relies heavily on written communication — Slack, email, documentation. Is their writing clear and concise?

**5. Environment and routine:** Do they have a workspace that supports focused work? Have they worked remotely before and established a productive routine?

### Remote-Specific Interview Questions

**1. "Describe your ideal remote work setup. What does your workspace look like and how do you structure your day?"**
Looking for: intentional routine, real workspace (not a couch), awareness of what they need to be productive.

**2. "Tell me about a time you were stuck on something and your manager was unavailable. What did you do?"**
Looking for: resourcefulness, independence, proactive problem-solving rather than waiting.

**3. "How do you decide what to work on first when you have multiple competing priorities and no one to ask?"**
Looking for: prioritization skills, comfort with autonomy, systematic thinking.

**4. "How do you stay motivated when working alone for extended periods?"**
Looking for: self-awareness, specific strategies rather than "I'm just naturally motivated."

**5. "Tell me about your experience with async communication tools — Slack, email, project management software. How do you make sure nothing falls through the cracks?"**
Looking for: systems and discipline around communication and task management.

## Part 3: What to Pay Remote Employees

### Should You Pay Based on Employee Location or Company Location?

This is one of the most debated questions in remote hiring. Both approaches are legitimate:

**Location-based pay:** The employee is paid based on the cost of labor in their geographic market. A remote engineer in Iowa earns less than the same role in San Francisco. This is what companies like Google and Meta do.

**Role-based pay:** The employee is paid based on the value of the role regardless of location. Fully distributed companies like GitLab and Basecamp use this approach. It simplifies equity and reduces resentment between team members.

For small businesses, location-based pay is typically more practical — it reflects real labor market rates and keeps compensation manageable.

## Part 4: Onboarding a Remote Employee You Have Never Met

Remote onboarding requires more structure than in-person onboarding — not less. The physical cues that help in-person employees absorb culture and find their footing do not exist remotely.

### Week 1: Connection and Orientation

**Day 1:**
- Welcome call with their direct manager (video, not just Slack)
- Introduction to the team via video call or recorded message
- Setup of all tools and access completed before day one (do not make them wait)
- Written document: "Your First 30 Days" — what they will learn, who they will meet, what success looks like

**Days 2–5:**
- Scheduled 1:1 calls with key teammates (15–30 minutes each)
- Access to documentation, processes, and training materials
- First small assigned task with a clear deliverable and deadline

### Weeks 2–4: Building Competence

- Daily async check-in (a 2-minute Slack update: what did I do, what will I do, any blockers)
- Weekly video 1:1 with direct manager (30 minutes)
- Gradual increase in responsibility as competence is demonstrated

### 30-Day Check-In

A structured conversation: What is clear? What is confusing? What do they need more of? What have they observed that they would change if they could? This check-in surfaces problems before they become departure decisions.

## FAQ

**Do I need a written remote work agreement?**
Yes. A written remote work agreement (either as part of the offer letter or a separate document) should specify: the employee's primary work location, equipment and expense policies, availability and communication expectations, and any requirements around returning equipment. This prevents disputes and sets clear expectations.

**Can I hire a remote employee in any country?**
Hiring internationally adds complexity — you either need a legal entity in that country, use an Employer of Record (EOR) service like Deel or Remote.com, or engage the person as an independent contractor (which has its own limitations). International hiring is feasible but requires specific legal infrastructure.

**How do I know if a remote employee is actually working?**
This is a common concern among first-time remote employers. The answer is: manage by outcomes, not activity. Define clear deliverables and deadlines. If deliverables are met on time and quality is high, the employee is working effectively — regardless of whether they are at their desk at 9am. Employee monitoring software is generally counterproductive and signals a lack of trust.

**What equipment should I provide to remote employees?**
At minimum: the hardware they need to do their job (laptop, monitor if required) and access to all necessary software. Many employers provide a one-time home office stipend ($500–$1,500) for remote employees to set up their workspace. Some states (California) require employers to reimburse employees for work-from-home expenses by law.

**How do I build culture with a fully remote team?**
Intentionally. Culture does not happen by accident in remote teams. Specific practices that work: regular all-hands video calls, casual virtual hangouts (optional game time, virtual coffee chats), public recognition of wins in team channels, and annual in-person retreats if budget allows. The companies with the strongest remote cultures invest in culture deliberately rather than assuming it will develop naturally.
