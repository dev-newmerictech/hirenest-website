## Why the Phone Screen Is the Most Underused Step in Hiring

Most small business owners either skip the phone screen entirely (jumping straight to in-person interviews) or treat it as a casual chat rather than a structured step. Both approaches waste time.

Skipping the screen means you spend 30–60 minutes with candidates in person only to discover they are unavailable for your required hours, want twice your budget, or fundamentally misunderstood the role.

An unstructured screen feels friendly but produces inconsistent information — you end up comparing candidates on different answers to different questions, making apples-to-apples comparison impossible.

A structured 10–15 minute phone screen eliminates both problems.

---

## What a Phone Screen Should Accomplish

By the end of a phone screen, you should know:

1. **Is their availability a real match?** Not "flexible" — do their actual hours align with yours?
2. **Are their compensation expectations in range?** No point advancing someone 3x over budget
3. **Do they have the baseline experience the role requires?**
4. **Is their communication clear and professional?** (Especially critical for customer-facing roles)
5. **Are they still genuinely interested after hearing the details?**

That is it. You do not need to evaluate culture fit, leadership style, or long-term career goals at this stage.

## The Universal Phone Screen Script (Adaptable to Any Role)

### Opening (1 minute)

*"Hi [Name], this is [Your Name] from [Company]. Thanks for applying for the [Job Title] position. I have about 10–15 minutes for a quick call to tell you more about the role and ask a few initial questions. Does that still work for you right now?"*

Always confirm they are available to talk. A candidate answering from a bathroom at their current job is not going to give you their best answers.

---

### Company and Role Overview (2 minutes)

*"Let me give you a quick overview of what we are looking for. [Company Name] is a [brief description]. The [Job Title] role involves [2–3 sentence summary of main responsibilities]. The hours are [specific hours and days] and it is a [full-time/part-time] role paying [salary range or hourly rate]."*

**Why include pay upfront:** Candidates who hear the compensation early and are clearly not interested will tell you — saving you both time. This is the single biggest time-saver in phone screens.

---

### 5 Core Questions (8–10 minutes)

**Question 1: Availability**
*"Based on what I just described — [specific hours and days] — is that something that works for your current schedule?"*

If yes, continue. If they hedge or say they would need to figure it out, ask directly: "What would make that difficult?" A clear answer is better than advancing someone with hidden scheduling constraints.

---

**Question 2: Compensation Confirmation**
*"The pay range for this role is [range]. Is that in line with what you are looking for?"*

If their expectations are significantly above your range, it is better to surface this now than after a full interview process. You can also use this to open a brief negotiation conversation if they are slightly above range.

---

**Question 3: Most Recent Experience**
*"Can you tell me briefly about your most recent role and what your main responsibilities were?"*

You are listening for: relevance to the role, the level of responsibility they held, and whether their description matches their resume. Inconsistencies between what they say and what is on their resume are worth noting.

---

**Question 4: Specific Role Requirement**
Tailor this to the most critical requirement of the role:

- For a janitor: *"This role involves [specific cleaning tasks]. What is your experience with that type of work?"*
- For a manager: *"Have you managed a team directly — meaning hiring, scheduling, and performance conversations? Tell me about that."*
- For a remote role: *"Have you worked remotely before? What does your home office setup look like?"*

---

**Question 5: Why Are They Leaving / Why This Role**
*"What is prompting your job search right now, and what specifically attracted you to this role?"*

Red flags: cannot explain why they are leaving, gives a negative answer about their current employer without any self-reflection, does not have a specific answer about why this role (suggests they are mass applying without reading postings).

Green flags: honest, straightforward answer about what they are looking for, specific mention of something about your company or role that attracted them.

---

### Candidate Questions and Close (2 minutes)

*"Those are my questions for now. Do you have any questions about the role or the company before we wrap up?"*

Then close:

*"Great — based on our conversation, I would like to [move you forward to an in-person interview / need a bit more time to review all candidates]. [If advancing:] I will send you some available times — does [day range] work generally? [If not advancing:] We are still reviewing candidates and I will be in touch within [timeframe]."*

## Scoring the Phone Screen

After the call, while it is fresh, rate the candidate on each question (1–3 scale):

- Availability confirmed: Yes/No
- Compensation aligned: Yes/Partial/No
- Relevant experience: Strong/Moderate/Weak
- Role-specific requirement: Strong/Moderate/Weak
- Motivation and interest: High/Moderate/Low

Any candidate with a "No" on availability or compensation should not advance unless there is a compelling reason to revisit those constraints. Score the rest and advance your top candidates.

## Common Phone Screen Mistakes

**Talking more than listening.** The screen should be 60–70% the candidate talking. If you spend the first 5 minutes describing the company in detail, you have wasted time you could have spent learning about the candidate.

**Not confirming scheduling upfront.** Starting a phone screen without confirming the candidate can talk freely results in distracted, rushed answers.

**Skipping the compensation question.** The most awkward question to ask is also the most time-saving. Ask it early.

**Making a final decision on the phone.** The phone screen is a filter, not a final evaluation. Advance candidates who pass — do not try to pick a winner at this stage.

## FAQ

**How long should a phone screen be?**
10–15 minutes is ideal for most roles. Senior roles may warrant 20 minutes. More than that and you are conducting a full interview over the phone — save the depth for when you can see the candidate.

**Should I video screen instead of phone screen?**
Either works. Video screens give you the added information of visual cues and give the candidate a better sense of your team. Phone screens are faster to schedule and conduct. For most hourly and service roles, phone is fine. For professional roles where communication style matters, video adds value.

**What if the candidate asks detailed questions I am not ready to answer?**
Be honest: "That is a great question — I want to make sure I give you accurate information. Can I follow up on that when we schedule the next step?" This is more credible than an uncertain or incorrect answer on the spot.

**Should I phone screen every applicant?**
For roles with 20+ applicants, phone screening your top 20–30% (after resume screening) is efficient. For lower-volume roles (under 10 applicants), you may choose to skip the phone screen and go directly to in-person interviews with all viable candidates.

**What if a great candidate is unavailable when I call?**
Leave a brief voicemail and send an email with your availability for a callback. If you do not hear back within 48 hours, send one follow-up. If still no response, move on — responsiveness during the hiring process is a preview of responsiveness on the job.
